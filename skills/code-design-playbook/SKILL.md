# Code Design Playbook

This is Code Computerlove's own design team curriculum, built from the internal training deck series the Experience team uses to train the studio in what it does and how it thinks. It is not generic UX theory — where Code has its own model (the Triple Diamond, Code's Persuasion Model, the hypothesis formats, the manifesto), that is Code's actual house view and should be used in preference to a generic textbook answer whenever the work is Code's.

**Two ways to use this skill:**

- **Reference mode (default):** when doing real design/research/product work — drafting a service design output, framing a research plan, running a sprint, writing personas, critiquing a layout — pull the relevant framework below and apply it as Code would, using Code's own terms (Triple Diamond, front stage/back stage, Code's Persuasion Model, etc.), not a generic substitute.
- **Training mode:** when explicitly asked to teach, onboard, or walk someone through a module (e.g. "run me through the research module," "teach the new starter about story mapping"), work through the module in the original teaching shape: state the "good to know" headlines first, explain the framework and why it matters, give the expert tips, then end with the module's reflection prompt — asking the learner to answer it in their own words rather than answering it for them. Don't dump all eleven modules at once; do one at a time and check they've landed before moving on.

Eleven modules, in the order a new starter would sensibly meet them:

1. Code's approach to service & product design
2. Product design process & methods

## 1. Code's approach to service & product design

**Good to know:** there's no industry-agreed definition of service design — in Code's own survey of 1,000 senior leaders/managers/directors, 62% said "I don't know" when asked what service design is, and only 3% of open responses matched Code's own definition. Almost 40% of respondents didn't know what impact their service design work had, and only 5% had a designated service design owner. Service design ownership is usually siloed to one department, and internal change takes longer to land than a customer-facing sprint — 70% of transformation projects fail (McKinsey).

**Code's manifesto:** Solve problems over predefined solutions. Experiences over designs. Product over project. Value over volume. Insights over opinion. Iterative over big bang. Adaptability over process.

**Product thinking (why "human centred" comes first):** every problem is tackled through three lenses at once — User (Desirability), Business (Viability), Technology (Feasibility).

**Code's definition of service design:** using a human-centred design approach to improve and invent the service experiences a person receives across end-to-end touchpoints, AND planning, organising and improving how a business delivers that service, using all the actors that influence it — people, technology, and process. As Lou Downe puts it: "Service design is 10% design, 90% is creating the conditions for design to happen."

**Front stage / back stage:** map any service as the customer-facing journeys (front stage) sitting on top of the channels, departments and systems that actually deliver them (back stage) — e.g. a "get a quote" journey is front stage; call centre, compliance, medical practice, marketing and digital teams delivering it are back stage. Zoom out far enough and you get from a single journey → a product → an account area → a full service → the whole organisation.

**JPSO — the evolution from product to service thinking:** Journey Design → Product Design → Service Design → Organisation Design. Digital experiences and rising customer expectations force businesses to reimagine service delivery, which in turn forces internal (organisational) change.

**OKRs in a service world:** an Objective (e.g. "launch a new service") breaks down into Outcomes (the work that gets you there) and Key Results (the measurable proof it worked — e.g. customers acquired, average customer value, NPS). Aligning OKRs across teams means each team's success becomes a signal that the business is hitting its larger goals.

**Change hypothesis format** (for service/organisational change specifically): *Because we observed (a problem) → causing (impact) → we believe making (a change) → will have (these outcomes) → and measured by (a metric).* Pair this with a **change story map** — story mapping applied to change initiatives, to visualise and prioritise the scope of a change programme the same way you would a product backlog.

**Code's view of change:** change is complex and uncertain, non-linear and continual, and can be divisive and emotional (research shows only ~38% of people find change exciting; the other ~62% default to fear/discomfort). Code's approach blends Design Thinking (define valuable problems from business need), Lean & Agile (rapid, iterative, feedback-driven), and human-centred practice (collaborative, co-created) rather than relying on classic change-management models alone.

**A people framework for growth:** breadth comes from expanding across the full cycle — journey → product → service experience → service optimisation → business change; depth comes from developing strategic thinking and leadership as you progress. The three "spaces" are: Problem space (Learn), Solution space (Make), Transformation space (Change).

**Everyone has a role in service design:** leaders set direction, define manageable goals, and hold teams accountable to clear service metrics; team leads bring people on the journey, stay outcome-focused, and experiment with ways of working; individuals invest in a service skillset and look for opportunities.

**Expert top tips:** technology should be selected to support the front-stage/back-stage service you want to deliver, not the other way round — engage technical teams at the start of a project and integrate them into the design process, don't wait until you know what to build. The average investment rarely stretches to a full change programme, so scope for that reality.

## 2. Product design process & methods

**Good to know:** design thinking embeds user empathy at the heart of the practice; product design is a team sport that works best mixing disciplines; design is a verb (doing), not a noun (the artefact); always know where you are in the process.

**Design thinking, in Code's terms:** (1) an approach to problem solving, (2) a set of creative thinking tools that give non-designers access to those tools, and (3) a cultural shift towards admitting nobody has all the answers — testing things and generating new insight rather than asserting a solution.

**The Triple Diamond:** Code extended the classic Double Diamond because it stops at delivery and doesn't take the work through the full product journey. The Triple Diamond adds a third, "transformation" space so continual change — measure and improve — is built into the design ethos from the start, not bolted on afterwards:
- **Problem space** (Discover → Define): spend time with the client and their customers, generate insight, define the problem, and agree how success will be measured.
- **Solution space** (Develop → Deliver): explore and make potential solutions, iterate and test them against business and customer needs using prototypes.
- **Transformation space** (Observe, Learn & Improve): deliver into a live environment, observe customers, measure results, and embed lasting change.

**Two things to always know where you are:** divergent vs convergent thinking (creating choices vs making choices — be deliberate about which phase you're in), and problem space vs solution space.

**Evolving design definitions**, narrowest to broadest: Journey (how we move users through a product to commercial success) → Product (define and make products of value) → Service experience (how the end-user engages with the service in its wider context) → Service optimisation (how well you're delivering, and where to improve) → Business change (the strategic purpose of a product/service — customer value, commercial return, market relevance).

**Product lens vs service lens**, across the same four disciplines: design thinking (end users and customer vs end users *and* internal users), modern engineering (an individual product's architecture vs the whole business's technical architecture and vendor selection), experiments & measurement (measuring digital products vs measuring products, service interactions *and* operational change), and product management (shipping via lean/agile vs coaching and change management, because you may be changing the business structure and team processes, not just the product).

**The methods toolkit**, roughly research-and-context through to build: contextual observation, user testing, expert review, user interviews, stakeholder analysis, remote research, personas, content strategy, product vision, a Day Zero workshop, lean hypotheses, OKRs, a product canvas, a product spike, an experience map, a roadmap, and a prototype/pattern library — feeding into a design sprint. A typical engagement runs Research → Definition → Delivery, opening with stakeholder interviews and an onboarding/vision session.

**Abstract → real, low-fi → hi-fi:** service design and art direction sit at the abstract end; design sprints and a pattern lab sit at the concrete, real end.

**Expert top tips:** always build measurement into the programme so impact can be demonstrated; content design and SEO insight are fundamental to good UX, not an afterthought; the phases of the process stay constant but the methods within them flex to the brief; a modern designer's job includes being the facilitator of the process and owning the programme, not just producing artefacts.

## 3. Research at Code

**Good to know:** good research helps you understand the user's mindset, not just their clicks; research never stops — the world changes, so the team's knowledge should too; research gives you insight over opinion when priority calls are hard to make; different methods answer different questions — talking to users tells you *why*, data tells you *what*.

**Research is a team sport** at Code: UX consultants and data analysts work alongside empowered product teams, not as a separate research function people wait on.

**Two types of research:** explorative (uncover needs and opportunities to lower the risk of where you invest effort) and validation (test assumptions and ideas to build confidence before you invest). Mapped onto the Triple Diamond: Discover/Define (problem space) is explorative; Develop/Deliver (solution space) is validation.

**Mixed methods, deliberately unbalanced:** qualitative research — engage ~10 people to uncover ~100 things; quantitative research — engage ~100 people to validate ~10 things. Use qualitative to find the right questions, quantitative to confirm the answer at scale.

**Research objective format:** *We are researching (what) … for (who) … so that (outcome).*

**Test hypothesis format** (the solution-space counterpart to the change hypothesis in module 1): *Because we have observed (insight) → we believe that (change) → will result in (effect) → confirmed when (measure).* An assumption-based hypothesis ("we think…") is higher risk and lower confidence than an insight-based one ("we have observed…") — the whole point of research is to move hypotheses from the high-risk end to the low-risk end before you commit build effort.

**Worked example — Park Christmas Savings:** objective was to grow sign-ups to a 12-month Christmas savings product and improve retention through the year. A 6-week programme combined customer interviews (first-timers, nil-payers, seasoned savers) with analytics/sales data review to build a data journey map of drop-offs. Findings — signing up for 12 months feels risky, Christmas is emotionally loaded, the real audience skewed toward single mums rather than the assumed "families," first-timers save for one big present while repeat savers try to cover the whole of Christmas — were turned directly into a slate of experiments (homepage emotional-vs-rational framing, simplified registration, condensed content) each written up against the hypothesis format above.

**Expert top tips:** research isn't a big, process-slowing task — treat it as something you build on continually, not a gate; it's a team sport, so share insight across disciplines so everyone's working from the same evidence; it's easy to slide into assumption-based experimentation, which is lower-confidence — notice when you're doing that; you can't do good research without a clear brief, defined users, and a plan for what you need to learn and do next.

## 4. Design sprints & ideation

**Good to know:** a design sprint tackles a big, foggy problem in a short, fixed space of time; it should always follow some research, never substitute for it, so the sprint is informed; the output is a prototype to articulate a concept, never a finished design; sprints are a good way to bring stakeholders along and pull insight from more knowledge-holders in the business. Design sprints are not agile sprints.

**The 5-day process** (research the week before, production after): **Understand** → **Diverge** → **Decide** (Converge) → **Prototype** → **Test/Validate**.

**Writing the challenge statement:** relevant (tied to team goals, concise), inspiring (framed as "How might we…"), and focused (names a target audience or segment).

**Methods by stage:**
- *Understand* — lightning talks on business goals, user interviews, field visits, experience maps, stakeholder maps, competitive overview → summarise learnings, generate insight statements, identify opportunities, pick the problem to tackle.
- *Diverge* — brainstorm, sketch 8 ideas in 5 minutes (Crazy 8s), sketch 1 idea in 5 minutes, sketch a storyboard in 10 minutes, a solution design workshop.
- *Decide* — zen voting, team review, thinking hats.
- *Prototype* — paper sketches, interactive sketches, designed click-throughs (Figma), HTML prototypes (Webflow).
- *Test* — user test/interview/observe, stakeholder feedback, technical feasibility check.

**Creative ideation techniques**, roughly in order of use: **moodboards** for visual inspiration (can be literal reference-gathering or more conceptual for campaigns); **mind-maps** to quickly make sense of everything you already know and view a problem from multiple angles; **Crazy 8s** — fold a page into 8, one idea per box in under a minute each, to force volume over quality under structure and time pressure; **T-Bars** — headline along the top, a sketch in the left panel, description/features/benefits in the right, aimed at someone who wasn't in the room being able to follow the idea; **storyboards** — for ideas that unfold as a journey over multiple steps/screens, where a T-Bar can't capture the sequence; simple sketches, not pretty ones, are the goal throughout.

**A practical Day 1 (Understand) exercise** used in workshop delivery: dissect the brief into what you already know, what you know you *don't* know, and what you want to know; sketch a rough product canvas; then turn the gaps into a research statement and plan using the same "we are researching X, for Y, so that Z" format as module 3, before moving into Diverge.

**Expert top tips:** divergent thinking is about creating choices before you make decisions — go for volume of ideas early, then find and polish the gems; the quality of a sprint's output is entirely dependent on the quality of its inputs; atmosphere matters — mindful facilitation of energy and psychological safety changes what a group is willing to say out loud; focus on making ideas clear and memorable before worrying about making them pretty.

## 5. Mapping (empathy / experience / service)

**Good to know:** mapping is best done collaboratively with other specialists, not solo; its main purpose is a *collective, shared* insight into the customer's experience, not a personal record; there are many map types and methods — map what matters for the decision at hand; a map is a piece of visual communication and should articulate the insight, not just decorate it.

**Three core map types:**
- **Empathy mapping** — an activity to surface deeper insight into customers' mindsets and considerations; can be grouped into mindset personas for design/targeting.
- **Experience mapping** — plots insight over time, aligned to how a customer actually experiences the service.
- **Service mapping** — plots insight over time like an experience map, but focused on the internal processes and how each step is served to the customer via people and technology (this is the front-stage/back-stage view from module 1, made concrete).

**Building an experience map — inputs:** web analytics, customer interviews exploring the service timeline, customer service team insight, technical systems and tools, and customer data capture points. **Outputs to map:** the stages a user moves through (e.g. research, consideration…), the order and detail of the steps within each stage, frustration points, the tools/systems in play at each step, opportunities, and — critically — the impact shown through data, not assertion.

**Expert top tips:** keep the map low-fidelity while you're still gathering input and feedback; consider a simple version and a detailed version for different stakeholder audiences; make sure the critical points are visually called out, not buried; back frustration/failure points with real business data to show impact, not just a sad-face icon.

## 6. User profiles

**Good to know:** profiles build empathy by getting the team to step into the user's world; they're an artefact for sharing what's been learned, not the research itself; they help you focus on a specific type of person and make defensible priority calls; the industry debate about personas is really about having the *right* profile type for the job at hand, not whether profiles work at all.

**Two axes decide which profile type to use.** Ask what you're trying to do — *find* customers vs *enable* customers already using the product/service — and whether you need an *empathy-based* or *task-based* view. That gives four profile types:

- **Demographic personas** — best for finding customers for highly targeted, branded experiences. Strengths: identify what motivates a targeted group, surface frustrations and needs, build team empathy for a person's situation. Limitations: say nothing about actual product-interaction behaviour, can create team bias, and people who share demographics often behave completely differently — treat demographic traits as a way in, not a behavioural explanation.
- **Data segments** — best for finding targeted customer groups at scale (cohort analysis, channel/device behaviour flows, new vs returning). Strengths: segment on real data, identify new prospects, personalise and target at scale, measure channel impact. Limitations: say nothing about *why* — no motivation, no behavioural explanation, no empathy.
- **Mindset profiles** — best when designing one core function for many different kinds of user. Strengths: group by how people think rather than who they are (non-biased, inclusive), surface frustrations/needs, identify functional requirements, and give context for behavioural and content design. Limitations: don't help with targeting, brand desirability, or finding new audiences.
- **Jobs to be done** — best when designing a complex service for many different users with complex needs. Strengths: frustrations, needs and desired outcomes, non-biased and inclusive. Limitations: like mindset profiles, don't help with targeting, brand desirability or new-audience discovery, and (unlike mindset profiles) don't build empathy on their own.

**Expert top tips:** profiles are not one-size-fits-all — pick the type that answers the question you actually have; keep them digestible and genuinely usable by the team, not a poster nobody reads; the data inside a profile should reflect the goal of the research that produced it; always create a profile with a specific purpose in mind, never as a generic exercise.

## 7. Story mapping & prioritisation

**Good to know:** story mapping visualises the scope of a product *and* prioritises the value of a release; releases can be sliced vertically (depth within one section) or horizontally (breadth across the whole scope, shallow); prioritisation sessions align teams on the metrics and rationale behind a decision, not just the decision itself; different prioritisation methods surface value for different things, but all of them should balance user need against business need.

**Anatomy of a story map:** themes (customer problems/needs/goals, taken from the roadmap) sit across the top, each broken into the steps a persona takes toward a goal, each step broken into features; features get grouped into releases (Release 1, 2, 3…) by priority, with a clear line showing what's in launch scope.

**Building one, in order:** (1) list core themes from the roadmap; (2) map customer flows to achieve each theme's goal (epics); (3) break flows into the features needed to deliver them; (4) add other necessary business features; (5) break big features into smaller ones; (6) prioritise everything against business *and* customer goals; (7) draw the launch scope line or allocate into release buckets.

**How it cascades:** Vision → Strategy → Roadmap (informs the themes) → OKRs (value-based key results, aligned with the roadmap) → the story map itself.

**Prioritisation frameworks** to choose between depending on what you need to see: **RICE** (Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort — Reach: how many people in a defined period; Impact: massive=3x/high=2x/medium=1x/low=0.5x/minimal=0.25x; Confidence: high=100%/medium=80%/low=50%; Effort: person-months, whole numbers, don't over-estimate to the decimal), **Impact/Effort**, **Evidence/Effort**, the **Kano model**, and the **Solution Quadrant model** (start bottom-left and eliminate the low-value/low-distinctiveness items, work toward top-right to agree what's most unique and valuable — often preferable to dot voting because nothing gets branded a "loser" in the room).

**Expert top tips:** prioritisation and story mapping are team activities, not something one person does alone and presents; a story map is never "done" — it's a living view that keeps improving around user and business need; themes should come from real user jobs and problems, not invented top-down, and everything else maps back to that; prioritise features on user *and* business value together with the effort to build them — never effort alone.

## 8. Persuasion principles

**Good to know:** users compare options before buying or signing up because they want the decision that carries the most value; persuasive design shows up most in advertising and e-commerce but applies to any site; it works by understanding real behaviour and needs, then nudging in the right direction; persuasive design takes behavioural insight — motivations, cognitive biases — and turns it into frameworks and patterns you can apply deliberately. ("60% of everything rung through the tills at IKEA is made up of impulse purchases" — Alan Penn, UCL Bartlett.)

**Code's Persuasion Model — four stages, in order, and each one gates the next:**
1. **Usability** — if a visitor can't convert, they won't convert. Interface elements need real affordance and to actually work; journeys need to be simple and low-friction; IA and navigation need to be obvious.
2. **Trust** — if a visitor doesn't trust the interface or the brand, they won't convert. Trust comes from brand, UI, content, or third-party sources you don't fully control; poor content or contradictory messaging actively undermines it.
3. **Emotion** — if you don't create the right emotional state, a visitor may not convert. Imagery and copy need to work together, stay consistent through the journey, and reinforce brand values throughout.
4. **Trigger** — if you don't nudge the customer at the right moment, they may not convert because they're unmotivated or distracted. Triggers need to sit at the most effective points in the journey, each step should drive toward the next, and calls to action must never compete with each other or with other interface elements.

**Persuasion techniques to apply within that model:** contrast (make the call to action obvious), serial position effect (people remember first and last most — order your options deliberately), limited choice (the fallacy-of-choice effect), affect heuristic (positive emotion shapes the decision), story (emotionally involves the user — speak to "you"), social proof (people trust something more readily if people like them have already engaged with it).

**How to run an assessment:** start from a real user scenario ("as a user who wants to X, I want to Y, so that Z"), then walk the actual journey against the model asking, at each step: is the user motivated to interact (Motivation/Emotion/Trigger), does the user trust the site enough to interact (Trust), and can the user interact with it effectively (Usability)? Findings get written up against those three headings, not as a vague list.

**Expert top tips:** beware dark patterns — persuasion design must serve the user's experience, not the business at the user's expense, and it's very easy to fall into that trap; use the framework to walk the journey with structure, or your findings will feel scattergun; never assess a single page in isolation — use the full scenario so you understand that page's role in the end-to-end journey; think beyond your own discipline's lens — design, content, and conversion all shape persuasion together. Draws on the Fogg Behaviour Model and Maslow's hierarchy of needs (not Material Design).

## 9. UX laws & industry patterns

**Good to know:** you can build on an existing body of research and behavioural study rather than reinventing interface decisions from scratch; visual affordance tells users quickly what they can actually do; UX heuristics give a shared standard to fall back on when no established pattern exists; persuasion/cognitive techniques must never be used as dark patterns to trick or manipulate users.

**Affordance** — the physical (or interface) properties of a thing should clearly communicate what you can do with it (Don Norman's classic example: doors). Well-designed products lean on this instinctively.

**UX patterns** are the web's version of affordance — commonly understood components that form the basis of a design. Stepping away from an established pattern means you now owe the user extra affordance to make the new thing understood, and different audiences need different amounts of it. Where no pattern exists, fall back on usability heuristics (Jakob Nielsen's 10 heuristics).

**Cognitive techniques** are mental shortcuts people's minds use to make decisions faster — often called persuasion techniques, and usable to make interfaces easier to understand and marketing more convincing, within the dark-pattern limit above.

**Four laws taught explicitly:**
- **Jakob's Law** — users spend most of their time on *other* sites, so they expect yours to behave the same way theirs already do; they transfer expectations from familiar products onto new, similar-looking ones. When changing something familiar, give users a way to keep using the familiar version for a limited time to reduce confusion.
- **Law of Proximity** — objects placed near each other are read as grouped and related; proximity helps users organise and understand information faster.
- **Miller's Law** — the average person holds about 7±2 items in working memory; chunk content into smaller groups to help people process and remember it, and remember that short-term capacity varies by individual and prior knowledge.
- **Serial Position Effect** — people best remember the first and last items in a series; put the least important items in the middle of a list, and position key actions (e.g. in navigation) at the far left and right to boost memorability.

**Expert top tips:** regularly use and study live websites to keep a feel for what users already expect in the market; lean on the established UX laws to shape layout and content structure rather than personal preference; limit how many cognitive techniques you stack in one place — they compete with each other if overused; don't design purely from your own personal mental model of how something should work.

## 10. Visual hierarchy & mobile-first design

**Good to know:** whitespace is your friend — don't cram content in close proximity or above the fold; users will scroll if the content is good, needed and visible, so don't over-optimise for what's "above the fold"; content is the building block of the product and should work at every size; mobile-first design keeps the team focused on the actual user journey rather than a desktop-first assumption.

**Responsive, mobile-first design:** responsive web design changes a site's appearance dynamically by screen size and orientation, using breakpoints (commonly based on browser width). The core principle is to *enhance up* from a solid, usable base state as screens get bigger — not to *reduce down* and compromise as they get smaller.

**Content-first design:** let the actual content of the site or product determine its design, not the other way round — this improves how components actually perform for CMS editors, and gives you much better feedback from peers and users when they're reacting to real content rather than dummy text (lorem ipsum).

**Vertical rhythm & grids:** vertical rhythm starts from a baseline and governs font size, line height and image sizing together; the common standard today is the 8-point grid. Consistent whitespace, built on that rhythm, is what lets content read with clarity.

**Page hierarchy and flow:** visual hierarchy creates a balanced layout that guides the eye top-to-bottom and left-to-right, letting it rest on what's actually important. Too much content given equal size, colour or shape makes everything equally hard to take in — hierarchy is what prevents that.

**Basic UI principles:** consistency of usage and layout structure is the key lever; a good UI's job is to reduce confusion and noise so the user can get to their goal, not to showcase the interface.

**Design systems:** a collection of reusable components governed by a clear set of standards, assembled to build any number of digital products consistently — not a new idea, but an evolution of the need to control brand execution at scale (rooted in Brad Frost's Atomic Design). Real-world examples Code references: Shopify Polaris, Sainsbury's Luna.

**Expert top tips:** visual hierarchy is fundamentally about creating balance, order and prominence among competing content on a page; consistency lets users apply a mental model to your content, which lowers cognitive load; always have the user need *and* the business need defined and agreed before you start laying out a page; design mobile-first, but don't stop there — always consider what happens as the layout scales up.

## 11. Inclusive design principles

**Good to know:** accessibility is just good usability for people in differing circumstances, not a separate discipline bolted on afterwards; conditions aren't always permanent — situations create temporary difficulty for anyone; inclusive design means putting people at the centre of the design process; people have conditions, but it's bad design decisions that exclude them, not the conditions themselves.

**Scale:** 14.1 million disabled people in the UK — roughly 1 in 5 (Family Resources Survey, 2019–20).

**Four pillars, and how they show up permanently *and* situationally:**
- **Vision** — permanent: blindness, partial blindness, colour blindness. Situational: sun glare affecting anyone's vision.
- **Motor** — permanent: conditions like dyspraxia, RSI, arthritis creating physical barriers. Situational: running or moving reduces the hit-target size someone can reliably use.
- **Cognition** — permanent: users at different developmental levels (e.g. Down syndrome, autism, dyslexia). Situational: a stressful situation reduces how much information anyone can take in at once.
- **Hearing** — permanent: partial or total hearing loss. Situational: no headphones to hand means anyone needs captions.

**WCAG ratings:** A (lowest, "good"), AA ("better" — the general standard businesses and public bodies should meet), AAA (highest, "best" — may be needed for specific audiences/areas even when AA is the baseline elsewhere).

**WCAG's four areas, each building on the last:** **Perceivable** (everyone must be able to consume the content in a way they can understand — familiar words, shorter sentences); **Operable** (everyone must be able to use the controls to access the content — remember reading online isn't linear, people scan and browse); **Understandable** (everyone must be able to understand the content — 7.1 million people in the UK have poor literacy); **Robust** (the site or service can't be broken by assistive technology).

**Design for the extremes:** source people from both ends of the extreme when researching — not just the mainstream middle — because a solution that works at the extremes tends to work for the mainstream too. Watch for "checklist" accessibility, where a requirement gets technically satisfied but the result is still genuinely broken for real users.

**Expert top tips:** walk in users' shoes to understand how they actually experience your designs, don't assume; content is a key, often-forgotten area of inclusive design — understanding someone's situation helps you prioritise what's actually needed; designing for the extremes gives you solutions that work for everyone, not just the edge case; a large share of inclusive design lives in the front-end build, not just the visual design.
